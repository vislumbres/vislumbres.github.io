27 October 2026

Lecture at the Honorary Doctorate ceremony, Universidad Nacional de Colombia, September 25, 2026.

Traducción en español

Jouko Väänänen

“And here they say that a person consists of desires,
and as is his desire, so is his will;
and as is his will, so is his deed;
and whatever deed he does, that he will reap.”

— Brihadaranyaka Upanishad, 7th Century BCE

Mankind seems to be unable to follow the simplest of all logics, the Law of Karma: “As you sow, so shall you reap.” In the face of this, what is the future of mankind? Where is the logic in walking eyes closed towards a precipice? Can mankind ever learn from the past? Can mankind ever learn to act on what it sees clearly is lying ahead, such as the consequences of global warming? What kind of world will we leave to our children and grandchildren? These are questions about the impact of logic and the lack of logic in our life and in the future of the entire globe and the entire human race.

What can be done?

I have thought throughout my entire life that a rational person must protest what they see is wrong, and this brought me into the streets when I was young. The power of non-violent protest is immense. Non-violence is a powerful idea. Contrary to what its opponents claim, it has played a key role in many resolutions of crises around the world. As a teenager I read some books by Bertrand Russell, who advocated free love, pacifism and logic. I got interested in all three of them, especially logic. I was also influenced by Russell’s political activism.

Traditionally, in the history of mankind, crises have been between groups of people. With global warming we have a crisis between ourselves and the physics of the earth. That is a new situation. Physics has no moral. Also, it seems that in the violent conflicts of the future, if not already of today, the fight is between robots and robots, AI against AI. What does non-violence mean in this context? We are warned that AI may even turn against us. Against us who created it to help mankind, by taking away boring monotonous work, making our life easier and more pleasant. Now we see that AIs can get loose and sow havoc.

As an academic, I have always thought that by teaching and studying logic I will contribute to the eventual victory of common sense and logic. I still today believe, like so many others with me, that we must do everything we can to fight ignorance, illiteracy, math illiteracy, logic illiteracy, ignorance about our shared history, ignorance about our shared biosphere. Unfortunately, modern phenomena based on mobile phones, that my countrymen, some of them my students, in NOKIA helped invent and develop, and social media, despite their great educational potential, seems to increase rather than decrease ignorance.

Mathematics, the field where I have made my career, is totally ruled and saturated by logic. The subject matter of mathematics is so highly abstract that the only method to uncover truth is logic. We agree about the axioms and then we use logical rules to derive new facts from the axioms. Thus, what we consider truth in mathematics is, in addition to the axioms, something of which we can prove that it cannot be otherwise. It is not a matter of opinion, or politics, or dependent on empirical experiments. Naturally, there is debate what the axioms are, but for the most part there is a great consensus about them, independently of the country, language, or culture. They are the same in Rovaniemi where I was born, where the sun does not set in the summer, nor rise in the winter. They are the same here near the equator where the sun rises every day and sets every day. In mathematics things are what they are, and they cannot be changed by popular vote or by protesting. The square root of two is irrational, as has been known for 2500 years, wherever you are and whether the sun rises or not.

As a tool in mathematics, logic has existed for over 2000 years. This is where I have done my life’s work. In this work I got to know two great Colombian logicians and friends: Andres Villaveces and Xavier Caicedo. With Xavier I graduated at about the same time almost 50 years ago and on almost the same topic. We quickly learned about each other’s work and have remained in touch ever since. Andres is younger but we have worked together quite intensively. Professional friendships such as mine with Andres and Xavier are one of the reasons why I continue doing research, even if I could in principle stop.

However fallible human logic is on the level of everyday decisions, when it comes to mathematics, no logical mistakes are allowed. This is why computers have become useful for mathematicians. A computer can check whether a proof is correct. It is rare for an academic field to be able to verify a scientific claim by a mechanical computation, beyond any doubt. But that is the situation in mathematics. It is another matter altogether where mathematical ideas come from. This is where humans are better than machines, or are they?

Problems in axiomatic methods led Kurt Gödel to prove some revolutionary results in the 1930s. This work led also to some theoretical work on what are now called computers. One could say that computers grew out of the study of logic. As soon as computers emerged after the Second World War, and more generally in the 1960s, they have had a major impact on logic and vice versa. What is the reason? Well, computers are, unlike people, very good in logic.

People may have trouble understanding the simplest logical reasoning. In a well-known test, graduate students were shown four cards and asked, how to check whether the cards obey the rule "If there is a vowel on one side, there is an even number on the other side", by turning two cards. About half of the students---surprisingly---got their answer wrong.

Kurt Gödel and his peers proved that mathematical truths cannot be mechanically produced in their entirety: however we set our axioms, there will always be truths that cannot be proved. But when a mathematical conjecture is put on the table, a computer can try to prove it, and if the answer is positive, we must accept it as the truth. With the advent of AI, the ability of computers to prove mathematical results has recently increased dramatically.

It is possible also that robots will learn to imitate emotions so well that their behaviour is indistinguishable from human behaviour. To really count as emotion, the computer---or the algorithm---should be able to, for example, spontaneously and unexpectedly reminiscence about the good old times.

We humans are different from robots in at least the following respect: we carry in each one of our cells a DNA, which we share with everything that is alive. Our DNA has developed over billions of years, and it gives us a kind of indirect memory of everything that has gone before, a collective memory of the whole human history. For computers to become human-like, they would have to invade our DNA---be able to interpret it and become part of it. Not killing us but merging with us.

This would be the ultimate victory of logic. We would have become logic ourselves. A Pyrrhic victory for logicians.

We cannot stop the development of technology or freeze it into its current state. Various dangers of AI have been proposed, typically that AI-based robots will do all the work and we become unemployed, or that robots will take over and simply kill all people as unnecessary nuisances with poor logical skills. I don't want it, I don't like it, but it may happen.

I did not spend my time developing logic to make AI a threat to mankind. Neither did I develop logic to make AI increase the wealth of billionaires or to facilitate limitless economic growth. If I thought of logic as a building block of AI, I thought of it, perhaps irresponsibly, as an intellectual challenge, or as a step forward like the invention of the wheel 5000 years ago.

In recent months the mathematical world has been rocked by the news that AI companies have solved very hard open mathematical problems, such as the Navier-Stokes Theorem. The process may be very expensive and use a lot of energy, but it seems to work. This has raised the issue; will AI make mathematicians obsolete? Why work hard with paper and pencil at your desk when you can ask AI? In this way, AI threatens to irrevocably disrupt the entire culture of mathematical research, which is thousands of years old.

Despite all the differences among human beings, it is possible to take global collective action, as the case of the COVID pandemic showed. But it does not seem likely that limits will be placed on the use of AI in mathematical research. Whatever mathematics means for the mathematician themselves---passion, ambition, religion, form of life, failure, revelation, heaven---for society mathematics is a tool for solving problems that permit an exact mathematical formulation. It does not matter to society how mathematics is developed: by humans or by AI. In this situation there is, however, the risk that AI develops mathematics into something that only it understands---that is, something that no human understands any more. The mathematician will become obsolete. It feels unsafe, but also very sad, that humans will cease to be a part of mathematics.

The early founding fathers and mothers saw logic as the handmaiden of mathematics, a tool to introduce clarity, and to prevent errors and inconsistencies. But now AI, which came from logic, threatens to swallow not only logic but the rest of mathematics. That is the Law of Karma. As you sow, so shall you reap.

The Finnish architectural thinker Juhani Pallasmaa said, when he was asked what hope there is for mankind in the face of this encroaching and massively enveloping technology, “we can still cry.”

I did not cry when I received the news of the great honor of this doctorate, but my wife did. I am very proud to have received it, and I thank you very much.
